1.9.0 2026-08-25
  • The drawing is now checked against the circuit it claims to be. Layout versus schematic walks the drawn shapes, works out what is electrically joined to what from the geometry alone, finds every transistor where poly crosses diffusion, and compares the nets that fall out against the netlist ngspice actually ran. All three amplifiers match. Every other check in this project asks whether the geometry is legal; this is the first one that asks whether it is right, and it is the only place the tool could have been wrong rather than merely incomplete.
  • A device became a device. Each transistor is drawn as the whole stack it takes to be one: the diffusion, the gate crossing it, the implant declaring the diffusion n-type or p-type, a column of contacts down the source and the drain, and the local interconnect over them. The gate got a contact too, on a pad above the channel, because poly cannot be contacted where it crosses the diffusion. Before this, every device had a gate that nothing could connect to.
  • The connectivity stopped being a list kept by hand. Two dictionaries recorded which devices shared a net and nothing checked them against the circuit that gets simulated. They are read out of the netlist now, terminal by terminal, which immediately showed what the hand-written version had been missing: the supply, the ground and both inputs were not in it at all. The measured interconnect went from 38 femtofarads to 82, because the nets that were never counted are now routed.
  • One metal layer cannot carry a circuit, and the old router had been pretending otherwise. On a single layer a stub coming down from a high track crosses every track below it, which is a short, and the rule checker could not see it, because two overlapping rectangles look like one wire. Metal1 now runs vertically and metal2 horizontally, joined only where a via says so.
  • The taps are drawn: an n-tap in the well and a p-tap in the substrate, contacted and tied to their rails. A well with nothing setting its voltage floats, and a floating well is how a CMOS circuit latches up and destroys itself. Drawing them found a violation that had been passing silently, because the checker had no rule for it: an n-diffusion has to stay 0.34 microns from an n-well and the row was leaving 0.09.
  • Twenty-two more rules, ten to thirty-two: the poly contact and its clearances, the contacts and the local interconnect, the implants, the vias between the layers and the metal that has to cover them, the second metal layer, the taps and the well spacings. Each is read from the PDK and each is tested against a shape built to break it. Two of them are not about dimensions at all: that every well holds a tap, and that every tap is contacted.
  • Placing the vias found that a device could be legal and unroutable. A transistor drawn to the transistor rules alone is 1.03 microns wide, but its source, gate and drain each need a via pad and the pads have to keep metal spacing apart, which needs 1.05. The footprint takes whichever binds.
  • A third topology, the folded cascode, which is V1.4 arriving late. The reason for it is not the circuit; it is the question a third one answers, whether the machinery built for the first two generalises. It did. Fourteen devices went through the placer, the router, the rule check and the comparison without one line of any of them changing, and came out clean on the first attempt. It earns its place too: 51.5 dB and 32.4 MHz against the five-transistor OTA's 37.0 dB and 14.3 MHz, for 230 microwatts against 69. The sizing was measured rather than chosen, over fifty-four bias points and a second sweep that found the n-channel cascode width moves the gain by seven decibels and nothing else comes close.
  • The layout panel reports two verdicts rather than one more row of numbers, because a rule check and a layout comparison answer different questions, and what was not checked is written underneath rather than left to be assumed. The drawing shows what the file holds, taps and both metal layers included, and the panel is called Layout rather than Floorplan, which it outgrew.
  • The loop closes. The strategist could design and measure and then it stopped: everything past that, drawing the geometry, checking it, comparing it against the netlist, running the foundry's deck, measuring the specs again with the interconnect loading them, was a thing a person did afterwards by clicking. It is one tool call now. A tool that turns a sentence into a sized schematic is a sizing tool; one that turns a sentence into geometry a foundry's own runset passes is a different claim, and the difference was that call.
  • The folded cascode can be designed to a spec like the other two, so the strategist chooses among three topologies rather than two. The seed is built on measurements rather than guesses: power is about 6.4 times the bias current times the supply, gain falls about 25 dB per decade of bias, and bandwidth rises about as its 0.7 power, all measured across a decade rather than assumed.
  • The search promptly beat the sizing that had been chosen by hand. Asked for 55 dB at 8 MHz under 80 microwatts, it returned 77.7 dB at 13.5 MHz on 78, which is twenty-six decibels more gain than the balanced preset at a third of the power. It was re-simulated on its own to confirm before being written down, and it is the High gain preset now.
  • The foundry's own deck runs now. KLayout is installed as machine tooling, and the SKY130 runset the PDK ships is pointed at the same GDS the tool already wrote. Nothing is reimplemented: the file goes over, the report comes back. All three amplifiers pass it, over the front-end, back-end and off-grid sections.
  • The first run of it failed geometry the hand checker had called clean, forty violations across three rules, all of them the same kind: a via needs more metal along one axis than it needs all round, and the hand checker had implemented the all-round half and stopped. The router draws to the directional rules now and the fast checker knows all three, thirty-two to thirty-five. That is the argument for running a real deck stated as an experiment rather than an opinion.
  • The hand checker stopped calling itself the answer. It is the inner loop, it says so in its own result and in the panel, and it points at the deck.
  • The comparison against the netlist stopped overstating. It compares transistors, because transistors are what the layout draws, and it never mentioned that the netlist also holds a bias current, a load, and for the op-amp the nulling resistor and compensation capacitor that make it stable. Those are listed by name now, in the panel, under what is not in the drawing.
  • KLayout also read the GDS the hand-written writer emits and agreed with it exactly: 762 shapes written, 762 read, right database unit, right layers, right bounding box. The writer has been checked against a tool that has no stake in the answer.
  • The manual has a contents, grouped the way the tool is used: getting a number, trusting it, building it. Seventeen sections in one column with no index is a document you scroll through hunting.
  • Two files stopped being one file each. circuits.py had grown to 2,456 lines doing four jobs and is now five modules of a few hundred each, verified by hashing every deck the tool can build before and after and finding all seventy-eight identical. app.js gave up its four measurement panels to their own files, and the three places that named every panel one by one became loops over what registered.
  • Splitting app.js found a bug that had been live and silent: stepValue was declared twice in one closure, once for arrow-key stepping and once to format a step metric, and the later declaration won for the whole scope. Pressing the up arrow on any form field replaced its contents with an em dash. Nothing failed and nothing logged. There is now a test that refuses any script declaring one name twice.
1.8.0 2026-08-25
  • The interconnect capacitance had been coming from a length guessed off the bounding box of the devices a net touches. Nothing was drawn, so nothing could be checked, and the number was an estimate wearing the clothes of a measurement. Each net is now routed as metal on its own track with a stub down onto every device it serves, and the length is taken from the geometry that was actually drawn.
  • The correction was material. The default op-amp carries 38 femtofarads of its own interconnect, not the 21 the estimate claimed, and the phase margin it costs goes from 0.17 degrees to 0.29. The estimate was optimistic by nearly half, because it counted the run across the row and not the stubs down onto devices tens of microns tall.
  • Two more rules for the metal that is now there to check: minimum width (met1.1) and minimum spacing (met1.2). Ten rules, and the routing is in the GDS: 2434 bytes for the op-amp, clean on all ten.
1.7.0 2026-08-25
  • The p-channel devices are drawn as p-channel devices. A PMOS has to sit in an n-well, and until now the floorplan drew every device the same way, which made the picture wrong rather than incomplete.
  • Drawing the well immediately broke a rule, which is the point of drawing it. Two separate n-wells have to stay 1.27 microns apart and PMOS scattered through the row put two of them 1.18 apart. The devices are now grouped by type, NMOS then PMOS, so the p-channel devices share one well. The grouping is not cosmetic and a test says why.
  • Three rules for the well: minimum width (nwell.1), the spacing between two that are not the same well (nwell.2a), and the surround the well owes the p-diffusion inside it (nwell.5).
1.6.0 2026-08-25
  • The drawn geometry is checked rather than assumed. A drawing nobody has looked at and a drawing checked against the numbers it was supposed to satisfy are different objects, and only one of them can be reported as either.
  • Every rule is tested twice, once against geometry that satisfies it and once against geometry built specifically to break it, because a checker that never fails is worse than no checker: it turns unchecked geometry into geometry someone believes.
  • The result carries its own coverage, so a clean pass can never be presented as more than the rules it actually covered, and a test refuses any wording in the module that would claim sign-off.
1.5.0 2026-08-25
  • The first answer to a question a schematic cannot ask: how much silicon does this sizing cost. The devices are placed in a row at the foundry’s own minimum diffusion spacing and drawn to scale. The default op-amp comes to 395.6 square microns, and the picture shows why: the 40 micron output driver is four times the width of anything else and sets the whole height.
  • Then the part that matters. Those positions give the wires between the devices a length, and metal of that length has a capacitance, so the capacitance is hung back on the nets and the same measurements run again. The default op-amp carries about 21 femtofarads of its own interconnect and loses about a fifth of a degree of phase margin to it. Measured, twice, not asserted once.
  • Every dimension and every capacitance is read out of the PDK’s technology file at run time: minimum poly width, the diffusion overhang a transistor needs, device spacing, metal width, and the capacitance of each layer per square micron and per micron of edge. Nothing is a remembered constant, and a test checks four of the rules against their published SKY130 values.
  • That test caught a real error while it was being written. The scalefactor lines in the technology file belong to its GDS output styles, not to its design rules, and using one made every dimension ten times too large: a 0.15 micron minimum gate came out as 1.5 microns. The rules are in nanometres, confirmed against four independent published minimums.
  • The geometry can now leave the tool. Download GDS writes the placement in the format every layout tool reads, on the real SKY130 layer numbers taken from the PDK: diffusion 65/20, poly 66/20. The writer is a hundred lines of standard library, because GDSII is a documented format and this project does not take dependencies it can write. Tests parse the stream back with a second, independent reader and check the geometry that comes out against the geometry that went in, including that the gate lands in the channel, overhangs it, and sits centred.
  • The geometry is now checked rather than assumed. Five rules read from the same technology file, each carrying the tag the foundry writes in its own error message: minimum poly width (poly.1a), minimum diffusion width (diff/tap.1), diffusion spacing (diff/tap.3), the diffusion overhang that makes a source and a drain (poly.7), and the poly overhang that keeps the channel from being shorted around the end of the gate (poly.8). Both amplifiers pass all five.
  • A checker that never fails is worse than no checker, so every rule is tested against a shape built to break it: a gate below the minimum width, a diffusion below its own, two devices drawn a tenth of a micron apart, a gate stopping inside its diffusion, and a device with too little diffusion for a source. All five are caught, and a legal device still passes, including one at the tightest sizing the form allows.
  • Writing the checker caught a remembered constant. The gate overhang was the number 0.13 typed into the geometry; it is the poly.8 rule, and it is now read from the PDK like everything else. It happened to be right, which is exactly why it was worth removing.
  • Five rules is not sign-off, and the result says so in its own words: the real deck has thousands and needs Magic or KLayout. What this is not, said plainly here and in the tool: a layout. There is no router, no design rule check, no layout versus schematic and no real extraction, because those need Magic and Netgen and neither runs on this machine. Devices are drawn as single fingers, so a wide transistor is drawn wide where a real layout would fold it. A test refuses any wording in the code that would claim otherwise.
1.4.0 2026-08-24
  • The page had grown wrong. Every analysis stacked under the form that only sets a circuit up, so the left column ran 6356 pixels against a 962 pixel right one and the whole page was 9262 pixels tall. The four analyses now share one tabbed section below the form, one open at a time, and the page is 2452 pixels: a bit over a quarter of what it was, with nothing removed.

  • A badge marked hidden had been rendering anyway, as a small empty box next to every reading, and the statistics row was holding thirteen pixels of space while hidden. Both were the same fault: a utility class that says display none can be beaten by any rule further down the file at equal specificity. It cannot be beaten now, and a test says so.
  • The step and rejection panels had been drawing every label on top of its own value. Their rows were wrapped in a div, which put both halves of a pair into a single cell of a grid that lays out its own children. The numbers were correct and unreadable. They are cells now, and a test refuses a wrapper in either renderer.
  • An empty frequency plot no longer reserves a full plot of empty ground before there is anything to draw in it.
1.3.0 2026-08-24
  • The rest of the datasheet. Both amplifiers now report CMRR, PSRR, input common-mode range and output swing, which are the numbers that tell the two topologies apart. The two-stage op-amp measures 76.5 dB of CMRR, 67.1 dB of PSRR and 1.24 V of range; the OTA measures 57.0, 36.2 and 0.91. The OTA needs no compensation and is worse at all three, which is a trade the strategist can now reason about instead of guessing.
  • An AC analysis excites every source in a netlist at once, so one copy of a circuit cannot be asked three questions and give three separable answers. The deck holds four identical copies instead, each with a single stimulus: a differential drive, a common-mode drive, a moving supply, and a buffer swept from ground to the supply. One library load, four answers, about half a minute.
  • The first attempt reported supply rejection as supply gain, which is impossible. The servo inductor that closes the loop at DC is an open circuit above it, which left the inverting gate floating and the answer meaningless. Holding that gate still for the signal, while leaving the DC servo alone, turned an absurd 29 dB at 1 kHz into 67 dB flat from 10 Hz upward.
  • A second plot, the DC transfer curve, with the ends of the measured range marked on it, so the range is a number you can see where it came from rather than one you have to trust.
  • Following means tracking, not agreeing. At the bottom of a sweep the amplifier is off and its output sits at zero, which matches a zero input to well inside any error tolerance. The measurement asks for unit slope as well, and a test builds exactly that trap to make sure it is not fallen into.
  • The device list now lives in one place per topology instead of one per testbench. Four builders had been about to hold four copies of the same eight transistors, and a copy that drifts is a testbench quietly measuring a different circuit. The refactor was checked by generating every existing netlist before and after and requiring them to be byte for byte identical.
  • The strategist gained both new measurements as tools, and its instructions now say which analysis each number comes from, so it cannot state a slew rate or a CMRR it has not measured.
1.2.0 2026-08-24
  • The first analysis here that is not a sweep. Both SKY130 amplifiers can now be measured as a unity-gain buffer hit with a 0.3 V step, which is a real closed loop with no servo in it, and the numbers that come back are the ones a sweep cannot give: slew rate, settling time and overshoot.
  • The measurement was checked against the physics before it was wired to anything. A two-stage amplifier should slew at its tail current over its compensation capacitor, 10.0 V/us for the default design; the measurement says 7.4, under that ceiling and in sight of it. Doubling the capacitor halved the measured rate and doubling the bias current raised it, both as the textbook says they should. A test pins the measurement between half the ceiling and the ceiling so a wrong answer cannot pass quietly.
  • Both edges are measured, because they need not match. The two-stage op-amp is slower rising, 7.43 against 7.65 V/us, and the OTA is slower falling, 6.71 against 7.00. The reported figure is the worse edge and both are shown, since a datasheet has to honour the slower one.
  • A new plot, the first in the time domain: output against time, with the moment of the step marked, drawn at the width it will occupy in the same idiom as everything else. The window sizes itself from the parts that set the slew, so a slower amplifier is watched for longer and its edge is still a visible slope rather than a vertical line.
  • Settling means settled. An earlier version of the measurement took the last sample as the final value and asked only whether it was near itself, which would call an output still climbing at the end of the window settled. It now checks the tail is flat first and answers “not in this window” when it is not, rather than inventing a number. The test that caught this is in the suite.
  • A trap in the parameter ranges is gone. Two widths on the op-amp offered up to 200 and 500 microns, but a SKY130 01v8 device accepts 100 and refuses 101, verified against the model library in both flavours. Anything above the limit failed with a raw simulator error, on the sweep as much as the step. The maxima now match what the device takes, and a test refuses any declared width that claims more.
  • What this does not do yet: the design iterator cannot target a slew rate, because a transient run costs a full simulation on top of the sweep. Making it a goal is the next step, not fine print.
1.1.0 2026-08-24
  • Faradaem has a face. The mark is the AE ligature itself, white on the ink ground, its outline lifted from IBM Plex Sans SemiBold and baked into path data so no font is needed to render it. It ships as the favicon, the touch icon, and an Open Graph card, all generated from one geometry by tools/make_brand.py so the sizes cannot drift apart.
  • The header logotype now sets the name with the AE ligature, the ligature in accent cyan, and a trademark symbol sized from the font’s own metrics: one third of the cap height, top aligned to the cap line, tucked inside the brand tracking. In prose the name stays plain ASCII Faradaem, and a test keeps the ligature from leaking anywhere else.
  • The home page opens like a product instead of a form: a landing that says what the tool does, one button that jumps to asking for a design, and the facts that matter in a single row. The header follows you down the page, and the schematic column holds still beside the controls while you scroll.
  • Every page now carries a descriptive title, an application name, and a full Open Graph and Twitter card block, so a shared link unfurls with the mark instead of a bare URL.
  • Fixed on phones: below 560 pixels the header lost its side padding and sat flush against the screen edge.
  • The circuit list is a row of separate bordered tabs, the chosen one filled in accent like the run button. Before, nine names ran together into one block of text.
  • Figures size themselves to what they contain. A drawing unit may grow to 1.15 pixels and may shrink to 0.85, no further either way: a three-part divider stops expanding instead of filling the column, and a dense circuit holds its size and scrolls sideways instead of collapsing into an unreadable thumbnail. Between those limits the drawing is centred in a full-width frame, and the page itself is wider, so the op-amp draws about a third larger than it did in 1.0.0.
  • Wires stop where they turn. Every wire was drawn with a round cap, which added half a stroke past each endpoint, so at every corner two segments overshot the turn and left a stub. Caps are flat now, and each transistor lead is one polyline that turns rather than two that meet.
  • The plot no longer collides with itself. Its axis titles sat inside the frames, right where a lowpass response runs, so the trace cut through the words; they are now rotated into the margin. Frequency ticks read 10 kHz instead of 10.00 kHz, which stops neighbouring decades from touching, and the two floating trace labels are gone. The plot is also drawn at the width it will occupy rather than stretched to fit, so its type renders at the size it was designed at.
  • The manual no longer claims only one circuit needs the process design kit; all three SKY130 circuits do, and the page now says so.
  • Faradaem can be published. tools/build_static.py writes the four pages, the assets, and the circuit catalogue into a folder any static host can serve, and vercel.json at the root builds it the same way from the command line or from a git push. The pages ask for the simulator first, fall back to the published catalogue when there is none, and drop into a static mode that keeps the live schematics and puts away every panel that would need a measured number, with a note at the top saying why. No number appears that nothing measured.
  • One test could fail for the wrong reason. It looked for stray simulation files across the whole system temp directory, so running the corner suite in a browser while the tests ran was enough to fail it, which says nothing about whether the run under test cleaned up. It now works in a private directory that no other Faradaem can reach.
1.0.0 2026-08-24
  • The loop is closed: specification in, verified schematic out. In the acceptance run, one sentence went into the panel and a strategist chose the two-stage topology for the gain requirement, generated a design from the targets, measured it, iterated to feasibility in six simulations, then verified it across all eleven PVT conditions.
  • Verified means verified: the shipped design holds every target at every corner. Worst gain 69.4 dB at low supply and hot, worst bandwidth 3.77 MHz at the slow corner, worst phase margin 63.3 degrees, worst power 67.2 microwatts, each named with the condition that caused it.
  • The whole flow is pinned by an end-to-end test that replays it with real tools, and the winning schematic is reproducible: rerunning its exact parameters lands on its exact numbers.
  • Deployment groundwork ships with it: a Dockerfile, continuous integration that runs the suite against a real ngspice on every push, and DEPLOY.md with the honest warnings about authentication and API credits.
  • What verified does not yet mean: layout, parasitic extraction, or silicon. Those are the next chapters of the plan, not fine print.
0.9.0 2026-08-24
  • PVT corners. A Robustness panel on the SKY130 circuits reruns the design across eleven conditions: five process corners, the supply and temperature extremes, and the two classic cross corners. The table names the worst case per metric and the condition that caused it. Measured on the OTA: unity-gain bandwidth falls from 14.3 MHz nominal to 11.4 MHz at slow, hot and starved.
  • Monte Carlo mismatch: twenty samples from the PDK's statistical models at the typical corner, each under its own seed, reported as mean, sigma, minimum and maximum. The PDK's bare mc section ships without device models, so mismatch at the typical corner is what Monte Carlo means here, and the manual says so.
  • The machinery is one hook: robustness runs edit the finished netlist text, the corner in the .lib line, the supply, the temperature card. No circuit builder learned anything.
  • The strategist gained run_corners and now verifies a finished design across PVT, reporting the worst case with its corner named.

One stage means no compensation, phase margin near 90 degrees, and better bandwidth per microwatt, at the price of gain: 37 dB at the base sizing against the two-stage's 71.5 dB.

0.6.1 2026-08-24

0.6.0 2026-08-24
  • The design iterator. Set targets for gain, bandwidth, phase margin and power, press Start, and a coordinate search sizes the circuit until every target is met. Each candidate is a full ngspice simulation; the measurements decide the next move, and nothing is ever predicted from a formula.
  • Verified end to end on the SKY130 op-amp: from a failing start at 28 degrees of phase margin, the search reached 73.4 dB gain, 6.8 MHz unity-gain bandwidth, 63 degrees of margin and 79 uW in nine simulations, and stopped the moment the spec was met.
  • The search runs as a server job. The page polls progress, shows the best point so far with a met or short verdict per target, and Stop actually stops it. Apply result loads the winning values and runs one confirming simulation, so what you see is measured, not remembered.
  • Candidates the simulator refuses, a bias that will not settle or a loop with no crossover, are recorded and skipped rather than ending the search.
0.5.0 2026-08-24

The op-amp tunes its bias current, input pair, output driver and compensation network. 0.4.0 2026-08-24
  • A hand-designed two-stage Miller op-amp on SKY130: eight transistors, a mirror-loaded input pair, a common-source second stage, and compensation with a zero-nulling resistor. The shipped sizing measures 71.5 dB of open-loop gain, 13.6 MHz unity-gain bandwidth, 74.6 degrees of phase margin and 156 uW.
  • Measured open loop with the classic DC servo: an enormous inductor closes the loop at DC so the operating point is a stable unity buffer, and the AC drive arrives through an enormous capacitor whose zero-ohm source holds the input node, so the loop is open by impedance at every swept frequency.
  • One sweep yields the whole datasheet line: open-loop gain, unity-gain bandwidth, phase margin, power, and the servoed output voltage.
  • Removing the nulling resistor costs twenty degrees of phase margin on the measured device. That is the right-half-plane zero doing exactly what the textbooks say it does, and there is a preset for it.
  • A sizing that does not bias, with the output stuck at a rail, is refused with a message naming the measured voltage and which widths to rebalance.
0.3.0 2026-08-24
  • Phase margin, measured from a real loop gain sweep. Every measurement before this one came from a closed loop response, and phase margin is not in there: it is a property of the loop gain, so the loop has to be opened.
  • A two-pole op-amp macromodel to measure it on. A single-pole loop has a phase margin of about 90 degrees no matter what you do to it, so it cannot tell a correct measurement from one that always returns 90. This one has a phase margin that is a real closed form and moves over a wide range.
  • The netlist carries two electrically separate copies of the circuit, one closed and one with the loop cut at the amplifier input. They share only ground, so one sweep measures both and the two responses cannot disagree about where the samples fell.
  • Measured against the closed form: phase margin agrees to 0.001 degrees, crossover to 0.05 percent, loop gain to 0.03 dB.
  • Gain margin is deliberately not reported. A two-pole loop never reaches 180 degrees of phase shift, so its gain margin is infinite and any number there would be an artifact of where the sweep stopped.
  • A user manual at /manual, covering how to run a simulation, how to read the result, what every warning means, and what to do when something goes wrong. Its tests check it against the code it documents, so it fails if a circuit is added without being written up.
  • Parameter combinations that cannot be swept now return 400 with a message saying which way to move, instead of 500.
